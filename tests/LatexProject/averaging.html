Introduction

We consider here optimal control problems which feature fast dependency on time. This is the case, for instance, if the problems depend on data which features high frequency phenomenons, i.e., features that happen at a much smaller time scale than the response time of the dynamics.

We consider here the solution of these control problems using the Pontryagin’s minimum principle. In this perspective, we have to solve a two point boundary value problem, be it through direct or indirect methods. To do that, we have to discretize the boundary problem. In our case, the size of the mesh is dictated by the high frequency features of the data, which is much smaller than the time scale of the dynamics. The question that is addressed in this paper is to find a solution method whose mesh size is dictated by the dynamics and not by the data.

When the fast data is periodic with respect to time, a classic approach to the solving of differential equations is averaging. Historically, the method of averaging was introduced to study the motion of celestial bodies by solving a simple two body equation which is perturbed by the influence of other bodies (see the section in about the history of averaging). As developed in , the framework was that of the perturbation of an orbit by the small influence of a periodic input. Averaging was then generalized in a geometric framework, notably in . A comprehensive book on the subject is . In this framework, the faster oscillating phenomenon is either periodic or satisfies some kind of ergodicity assumption.

The previous references consider a non controlled dynamical systems. We turn our attention now to controlled systems, and, in particular, optimal control problems (OCPs).

In this perspective, we add to the differential equation an extra parameter u which is the open loop control of the system. If we have non-linearities in the dynamics, common sense dictates that averaging be performed after the control is introduced, by contrast to the approach where the control would be introduced after the differential system is averaged. Indeed, the high frequency content of the control may interact with the high frequency content of the data; therefore the averaging must be applied once this interaction is taken into account.

In the case of ordinary differential equations, the approximation value of the averaging method is determined by the proximity of the nominal and averaged trajectories.

However, in the case of controlled systems, there are as many trajectories to consider as there are different controls. Fortunately, in optimal control, there is a simple way to evaluate the performance of an approximation method:

  • We first consider the optimal cost J0 for the nominal control system.

  • On the other hand, we considered the optimal control u* for the approximated control system, and we use it to control the nominal system. This generates a cost J*.

  • Naturally, J* is greater than J0. As a consequence, we can evaluate the quality of the approximation method by considering the loss of optimality J* − J0 which is non negative.

Let us review some previous work on this topic.

An early work applies averaging to two point boundary value problems, but its application to optimal control is limited to what is essentially the linear quadratic case. In , the method of averaging is applied to optimal control, both in open loop (the system is then periodic with respect to the fast time), and in closed loop (which is a study of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation under an ergodicity assumption). The study of the HJB equation is improved in . Observe that, in these two references, the horizon is finite and the oscillatory input is fast.

References consider an optimal control problem “in the long run”, that is, with slow and “normal” time scales, using averaging techniques. The convergence of the optimal cost is proved, but there is no estimation of the loss of optimality that the approximation method produces.

Reference constructs the method of averaging for controlled systems on time scales, and determines the algorithm of correspondence of controls over the original and averaged systems. It is an interesting work, but we shall prove in this article that the order of efficiency of the method is under-estimated (it should be two).

As we pointed out before, the application of averaging to celestial mechanics is a classic. It has been naturally transposed to the context of optimal control. Specifically, the optimal control of low thrust engines in space have been studied from a geometric view point and in the periodic case. Such a approach has been adopted in . Averaging has also been used for similar problems in a spirit that is close to . All of these references assume that the fast dynamics are either periodic or satisfy an ergodicity assumption.

There are applications of averaging in optimal control that are more data driven (by contrast to autonomous celestial mechanics). Energy management of thermal systems consider the optimal control of the temperature of buildings . Naturally, this heavily depends of the weather data, which naturally features fast oscillating components (with respect to the building’s inertia).

Reference considers a buoy which is subject to the oscillating influence of waves.

Averaging has been also used is stochastic optimal control, notably of Markov chains (see for instance ). Indeed, when there exists a cycle in a discrete state Markov chain that has high transition probabilities, then this cycle is gone through very fast and averaging can be applied. An alternative to the classical averaging scheme is ergodic theory .

In this article, we consider optimal control problems which are influenced by some fast perturbation data (with respect to the time constant of the system). The approach is somehow different from what exists in the litterature.

Here we consider, as far as rapidly oscillating inputs are considered, that the behavior of the system is similar to the response of a single integrator. This is why we first define our averaging method by the effect of integration on general signals. The averaged signal is defined by its successive averages on consecutive intervals. This transformation can be from various points of view:

  • In the frequency domain, this averaging method is low pass filter, although not a very good one . Indeed, it is a Finite Impulse Response filter, and thus its transfer function is not compactly supported.

  • In the time domain, this averaging operator is an orthogonal projection on the Haar basis. More efficient approximation methods exist in the wavelet framework ; however specific tools must be used for compactly supported signals and, in practice, their have boundary effects that are not desirable when noise is present.

  • From a statistical point of view, this method can viewed as the computation of a sequence of empirical expectations, typically for a non stationnary signal. This why we call our method empirical averaging.

From this approximation technique we derive a similar one for ordinary differential equations, control systems, and ultimately optimal control problems.

In all of these contexts, the performance of the averaging method is evaluated by the difference between the output of the reference signal and the averaged one, through an integrator. The maximum value for this difference is called α.

It is a very simple thing to evaluate and as a consequence, it may be applied to a wide range of situations.

An important feature of this problem is that the mesh size of the two point boundary value problem is not dictated by the fast data, but rather by the time constant of the system (which is larger than the width of the averaging interval). The minimization consists in the minimization of an averaged Hamiltonian.

This numerical technique has already been applied to the optimal control of a low thrust space engine that must reach its final orbit; the controls in the Keplerian case (where periodicity exists) and in the non Keplerian case (where the influence of the moon and of the sun breaks the periodicity) have been successfully computed .

Problem statement

Nominal Problem

We wish to solve the following Optimal Control Problem (OCP) : minu∫0TL(x,u,t)dt where x is is a finite dimensional state which satisfies the dynamics : $$\frac{dx}{dt}=f(x,u,t) \; , \; x(0)=\chi_{0} \label{dynamics}$$ where u is an integrable finite dimensional and unconstrained control. The assumptions on f and L are given in section 5.1.

Averaged Problem

If the dependency of f or L with respect to time contains fast oscillations, the nominal problem may be difficult to solve because the mesh size is dictated by the fast oscillation rate. To avoid this, we define an averaged problem with the help of a very simple low pass filter. This averaging method applies to any kind of time-varying (or data-driven) system.

Filtering of a signal by windowed averaging

We consider a signal defined on an interval and split this interval into N consecutive intervals. On each interval we compute the average of the signal.

Definition 1. Let N an integer, and g an integrable function on [0,T]. Let us subdivide [0,T] into N intervals [tk,tk + 1], with: $$t_{k}=k\frac{T}{N} \; , \; k = 0..N \label{intervals}$$ The averages of g on these intervals define the low pass filter LP on g: $$LP[g](t) = \frac{1}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}g(s)ds \; , \; t\in \left[t_{k},t_{k+1}\right) \; , \; k=0..N-1 \label{LdefP}$$ The difference g − LP[g], that is the averaging error, defines the high pass filter HP on g: HP = Id − LP We then denote I[g] the antiderivative of the averaging error: I[g](t) = ∫0tHP[g](s)ds , t ∈ [0,T]

We show now that the upper bound of the function I[g] is small when N is big and g is bounded.

Proposition 1. The following bound holds for any bounded function g and any t ∈ [0,T]: $$|I[g](t)| \leq 2 \|g\|_{\infty}\frac{T}{N} \label{BoundI}$$

Proof. Let us first prove that for any k = 0..N ∫tktk + 1HP[g](t)dt = 0 Indeed: $$\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}HP[g](t)dt = \int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}g(t)dt -\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}} \left[\frac{1}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}g(s)ds]\right]dt = 0$$ Hence, for t ∈ [tktk + 1[: I[g](t) = ∫tktHP[g](s)ds But |LP[g](s)| ≤ ∥g∥∞, so that |HP[g](s)| ≤ 2∥g∥∞. This gives finally: $$|I[g](t)|\leq 2 \|g\|_{\infty}(t-t_{n+1}) \leq 2 \|g\|_{\infty}\frac{T}{N}$$ ◻

Thus we may define a small number α that represents how close the signal g is closed to its averaged signal LP[g]: α = ∥I[g]∥∞

Example 1

Let us suppose that g is periodical with period $\frac{T}{N}$. Then LP[g] is constant equal to the mean of g over a period. The function HP[g] is a periodic signal with 0 average. The function I[g] is the periodic antiderivative of HP(g) with value 0 at 0. Its upper bound α is of order $\frac{T}{N}$.

Example 2

Let us suppose that g is periodical with period ϵ a small divisor of T, and N = 1. Then LP[g] is constant equal to the mean of g over the many periods in [0,T]. The function HP[g] is a periodic signal of small period ϵ with 0 average. The function I[g] is the periodic antiderivative of HP(g) with value 0 at 0. Its upper bound α is of order ϵ. This example shows that α can be small with seemingly large intervals.

Averaging errors for ordinary differential equation

Let us consider the ordinary differential equation (ODE): $$\frac{dx}{dt}=f(x,t) \; , \; x(0)=\chi_{0} \label{ODE}$$ with f Lipschitz with respect to x and integrable with respect to t.

The averaged ODE is defined as: $$\frac{dx_{0}}{dt}=LP[f](x_{0},t) \; , \; x_{0}(0)=\chi_{0} \label{aveODE}$$ where the low-pass filter LP on a general function g(x,t) is defined as: $$LP[g](\xi,t) = \frac{1}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}} \int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}g(\xi,s)ds \; , \; t \in [t_{k},t_{k+1}) \; , \; k=0..N-1 \label{avedDynODE}$$ As a summary, LP[g] averages g with respect to time on rectangular adjacent windows, leaving the state variable unchanged.

The state x0 is well defined and bounded because f, and thus LP[f], is Lipschitz.

We consider now x0 the solution of ([aveODE]) and g(x,t) a general function of the state and time. We establish a bound in the following propostion that will be used for the definition of the small parameter α, which will measure in section 2.2.6 the averaging error for optimal control problems.

Proposition 2. Let g(x,t) be bounded Lipschitz in x with a Lipschitz constant λg, and let’s suppose that f(x,t) is bounded. Let I[g,x0] the function of t defined by: I[g,x0](t) = ∫0t[g(x0(s),s)−LP[g](x0(s),s)]ds where x0 is the solution of the averaged ODE ([aveODE]) Then the following bound holds: $$|I[g](t)| \leq 2 (\|g\|_{\infty} + \lambda_{g}T \|f\|_{\infty})\frac{T}{N} \label{BoundODE}$$

Proof. see appendix 9. ◻

We now estimate the distance between the trajectories obtained respectively from the nominal and averaged system.

Proposition 3. Let x defined by the ODE ([ODE]) and let x0 defined by the averaged ODE ([aveODE]). Let λ the Lipschitz constant of f in x and define α = supt ∈ [0,T](|I[f](t)|) Then the following bound holds: $$\|x-x_{0}\|_{\infty} \leq \alpha \frac{e^{\lambda T}-1}{\lambda}$$

In other words, the averaging error ∥x − x0∥∞ is in first order in α, that is small when N is big because f is Lipschitz and thus bounded on [0,T].

Proof. We have $$\begin{aligned} |x(t)-x_{0}(t)| & = & \left|\int_{0}^{t} f(x(s),s) -f(x_{0}(s),s) + f(x_{0}(s),s) - LP[f](x_{0}(s)\right| ds\\ & \leq & \lambda\int_{0}^{t}|x(s)-x_{0}(s)|ds +\alpha\\ \end{aligned}$$ which leads to the result thanks to the Gronwall lemma. ◻

Averaging of a controlled system

In the scope of this article, we define, for the integer N > 0, and any functions u(t) and g(x,u(t),t), an averaged function LP[g,u] $$\label{average} \begin{split} LP[g,u](x,t)= & \frac{N}{T}\int_{\frac{kT}{N}}^{\frac{(k+1)T}{N}}g(x,u(s),s)ds\\ & \textrm{ for } t\in\left[ \frac{kT}{N},\frac{(k+1)T}{N}\right) \textrm{ and } k\in[0,N-1] \end{split}$$ As a summary, LP[g] averages g with respect to time (this includes the open loop control) on rectangular adjacent windows, leaving the state (or costate) unchanged.

Statement of the averaged problem

We define the averaged OCP which consists in minimizing the cost J0(v) = ∫0TLP[L,v](y(s),s)ds where y is the state defined by $$\frac{dy}{dt}=LP[f,v](y,t)\; , \; y(0)=\xi_{0} \label{averagedyn}$$ The system ([averagedyn]) is a well defined differential equation.

Assumption 1. The averaged OCP admits an optimal control u0 with a corresponding trajectory x0.

The optimal trajectory x0 is defined by the ODE: $$\frac{dx_{0}}{dt}=LP[f,u_{0}](x_{0},t)\; , \; x_{0}(0)=\xi_{0} \label{aveOptTraj}$$

Stationnarity condition for the averaged problem

Theorem 1. Let H be the Hamiltonian of the nominal problem: H(x,u,p,t) = L(x,u,t) + p f(x,u,t) Let p0 be the costate of the averaged problem, defined along the optimal trajectory x0 by the backwards differential equation: $$\frac{dp_{0}}{dt}=-LP\left[\frac{\partial H}{\partial x},u_{0}\right] (x_{0},p_{0},t)\; , \; p_{0}(T)=0 \label{aveCostate}$$ Then the following stationarity condition holds: $$\frac{\partial H}{\partial u} (x_{0}(t),u_{0}(t),p_{0}(t),t)=0 \; a.e. \label{StatCond}$$ where a.e. stands for almost everywhere in t ∈ [0,T].

Proof. see appendix 10 ◻

Introduction of a small α

We want to define a small α as we have done for signals and differential equations.

For a function g(x,u,t), the difference g(x0(t),u0(t),t) − LP]g, u0](x0(t),t) is the result of high pass filtering HP]g, u](x0(t),t). Let’s define the antiderivative: I[g,x0,u0](t) = ∫0tHP[g,u0](x0(s),s)ds Then, as a consequence of Proposition 2, the following bound holds: $$|I[g,x_{0},u_{0}](t)| \le (2 \|g\|_{\infty} + \lambda_{g}T \|f\|_{\infty})\frac{T}{N} \label{boundIcontrol}$$ In other words, ∥I[g,x0,u0]∥∞ can be made small if N is large, f is bounded and g is bounded and Lipschitz.

Let’s define similarly the backwards antiderivative IT[g,x0,u0](t) = ∫tTHP[g,u0](x0(s),s)ds Then, with a similar proof, the same inequality holds: $$|I^{T}[g,x_{0},u_{0}](t)| \le (2 \|g\|_{\infty} + \lambda_{g}T \|f\|_{\infty})\frac{T}{N} \label{boundIcontrol2}$$ Consequently, ∥IT[g,x0,u0]∥∞ can also be made small if N is large, f is bounded and g is bounded and Lipschitz.

Definition 2. For the rest of that document, we consider the small number α defined as: $$\alpha = \sup \left( \| I[f,x_{0},u_{0}]\|_{\infty} \; , \; \left\|I^{T}\left[\frac{\partial H}{\partial x},(x_{0},p_{0}),u_{0}\right]\right\| _{\infty} \right) \label{alpha}$$

We shall see that α measures how close the nominal and averaged problems are. Observe that α is small, for instance, when N is big and assumption 2 below holds (bounded functions and their derivatives).

Observe that that α depends only on the solution of the averaged problem.

Numerical perspective

Technically, the proof of our main approximation result is based on the Pontryagin principle. This is a theoretical result which holds independently of the actual numerical method used to solve the nominal (resp. averaged) problem.

Now that we have stated what the averaged optimal control problem is, we can begin to evaluate its benefit when solving numerically a general optimal control problem.

Let us consider a direct method for the solving of problem ([cost],[dynamics]). To do so, we discretize the dynamics into xk + 1 = xk + δt f(xk,uk,tk) In order to represent accurately the influence of the data on the system, the discretization step δt must be a suitable sampling rate for the data. In this article’s framework, δt is much smaller than the time constant of the system. The approximation of the cost is obtained by using a quadrature rule for the integral. Without using extra knowledge on the system, the time step of the quadrature is δt.

In this framework, the optimal control problem becomes a finite dimensional optimization problem with a finite number of equality constraints.

We consider now a similar direct method to solve the averaged problem ([averagecost],[averagedyn]). To avoid confusion, we denote by capital TK the boundaries of the averaging intervals, and XK denotes the state of the discretized averaged system. The dynamics of X is XK + 1 = XK + ∫TKTK + 1f(XK,u(s),s)ds where a suitable quadrature rule is used to compute the integral. Consistently with the discretization ([discretized]), we consider the sampling rate of the control and data to be δt. The computation of the cost is handled similarly.

We see that, while ([discretized]) only consider the differential aspect of the dynamics, the averaged dynamics ([discretizedAverage]) incorporates the integral action of a dynamical system.

Let us now consider the benefit of solving the averaged problem versus solving the nominal problem. To make things even simpler, let us use a Riemann integral for the computation of ([discretizedAverage]) and define $$R=\frac{T_{K+1}-T_{K}}{\delta t}$$ We assume that R is a (large) integer. Indeed, δt is dictated by the sampling rate of the data, while TK + 1 − TK is dictated by the time constant of the system, which is much larger.

We see that the number of control variables uk is the same in both problems. However, the number of constraints ([discretizedAverage]) for the averaged problem is R times smaller than the number of constraints ([discretized]) for the original problem. This is the main benefit of using averaging to solve the optimal control problem with direct methods.

Finally, observe that the definition of the averaged costate ([aveCostate]) indicates what should be an indirect method, i.e. based on the Pontryagin principle, for the solving of the averaged problem. In this case, the benefit of using the averaging method is a coarser grid for the two point boundary value problem.

Applicative Example

We describe here the example of the orbit transfer of low thrust space engine in the non keplerian case . Ionic thrust engines work as follows: instead of using a chemichal reaction to eject material, ions are ejected from the engines using an eletro-magnetic field. The resulting thrust is much lower than with a classical engine, but the required mass for the same acceleration is proportionnally smaller (within the operating range).

Description of the problem

A geostationary satellite (communication, meteo, TV) can not reach its orbit directly with a classical launcher. It is rather set on a transfer elliptic orbit and then brought to its geostationnary orbit by its own means: see figure 1.

The orbit transfer is done by a ionic thrust engine rather than a conventional engine in order to save the mass of fuel and oxygen needed by a conventional engine. Thus the transfer is slow and needs a lot of orbits (about 90).

The optimisation criteria is either the time length of the orbit transfer or the mass loss and thus the initial mass need.

Characteristics of the optimal control problem

The dynamics of the satellite is very close to an orbital movement, so that the parameters may be divided into:

  • orbital slow parameters,

  • angular speed position on the orbit,

  • plus the mass, that is also slowly decreasing.

Thus the problem is ill conditioned. Indeed, the optimal control probem, with a 90 orbits horizon, needs a very great amount of time samples, so that the two boundary value problem can not be reasonably numerically solved.

Averaging on successive orbits

In order to take advantage of the almost orbital movement, an averaging process is done on each orbit, so that the time sampling can be coarser and the optimal control problem can be numerically solved.

Note that a time conversion is done, defining the angular positin on the orbit as the new “time”, so that the dynamic is nearly periodic of period 2π.

Keplerian Case

When only the earh gravity is taken into account, we are in the so called Kepkerian case, and the dynmic is periodic, with the low thrust of the ionic engine, the control parameter, as only perturbation. In that case, a “classical” averaging scheme on successive orbits can be performed.

Result

The solution of the averaged problem is a progressive modification of the orbit until it is the geastationary one, as shown on Figure 2.

The costs of the orbital tranfer are respectively a duration of 1235.4457 days and a mass loss of 208.7603 kilos.

The thrust strategy is divide into two phases, with a transition at about half the orbital transfer:

  • an acceleration phase, where the satellite is accelerated at its apogee and perigee, so that apogee and perigee heights are both increaased,

  • a deceleration phase, where the satellite is decelerated at its apogee and accelerated at its perigee, so that apogee and perigee heights are respectively decreased and increased.

Non-Keplerian Case

Moon and sun gravity forces can not be neglected in the context of a low thrust transfer. So that they are taken into account in a further version of the optimal orbit transfer search.

But this results in a non-Keplerian problem, that is only nearly periodic and not exactly periodic in the angular position on the orbit.

This is why an windowed averaging scheme on 2π wide successive windows is interesting. But we can wonder whether that averaging scheme leads to costs and thrust strategy that are close to the Keplerian case.

The answer is yes:

  • the costs of the optimal averaged problem of orbital transfer are respectively a duration of 135.0423 days and a mass loss of 208.1386 kilos, that are very close to the corresponding costs in the Keplerian case.

  • The thrust strategy is divided into the same two phases, acceleration and then deceleration, as in the Keplerian phase

This is true even though the costates are quite different in the different configurations.

Conclusion of the example

The widowed averaging scheme proves to be as affective in the periodic case (Keplerian) than in the non-periodic case (non-Keplerian case with moon and sum gravity forces). And this is very useful in order to have an accurate and numerically solvable optimal orbit transfer problem. The fact that the solution is nearly optimal, with close to each other trajectories, is the result of the present paper.

Conclusion

We have shown that averaging on consecutive intervals is a flexible and efficient way to approximate signals while getting rid of rapidly varying features. We have extended this method to ordinary differential equations, control systems and optimal control problems. This provides a very flexible framework for the approximation of optimal control problems with rapidly varying features.

Indeed, if α is a measure of the averaging error on the dynamics, we have shown that the level of suboptimality due to the use of the solution of the averaged problem into the original control system is bound by some kα2, where k depends on very general bounds on the dynamics and on the convexity of the system.

This makes it possible to use a coarser time mesh in the numerical computation of the solution of the optimal control problem, as explained in section 2.3.

However, this does not decrease the number of optimizations of the Hamiltonian. This stresses, if needed, the requirement for fast minization routines in this kind of optimal control problem solving.

As a final note, we can observe that the control here is not constrained. However, using an interior penalty approach as in should be a reasonable method to generalize this article’s result to the constrained input case.

Proof of the stationarity condition of the averaged problem

Averaged two boundaries problem

Let u0 be the optimal control of the averaged problem ([averagedyn]) and let x0 be the corresponding trajectory. x0 is defined by the ODE with initial condition ([aveOptTraj]). Formal expansions in α

We formally expand the state, costate and control variables with respect to α. This step leads to the definitions of the various terms which are used throughout the article to finally yield the bounds stated in the main theorem.

Expansion of the state x

The state variable x is the solution of the original dynamics equation ([dynamics]) driven by the control u. Let us define a formal expansion of x and u with regards to α: x = x0 + αx1 u = u0 + αu1 Note that the redundant definitions of x0 and u0 are consistent, as will be seen later.
